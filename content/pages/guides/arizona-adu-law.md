**Last reviewed: October 23, 2026.** Arizona law changed twice in two years — HB 2720 in 2024 and HB 2928 in 2025 — and both changes favor homeowners who want to build a casita, guest house, or in-law suite. This guide explains what the law actually says, with citations to the statutes and city rules so you can check every claim yourself.

*This guide is general information, not legal advice. Statutes get amended and city ordinances vary; confirm the current rules with your city or town — or with an attorney — before making decisions about your property.*

## What Is Arizona's ADU Law?

In May 2024, Arizona adopted **HB 2720** ([Laws 2024, Chapter 196](https://www.azleg.gov/legtext/56leg/2r/laws/0196.htm), approved by the Governor on May 21, 2024). The bill created a new statute, **[A.R.S. § 9-461.18](https://www.azleg.gov/ars/9/00461-18.htm)**, that takes the ADU question away from each city's discretion and sets a statewide floor: if your city has more than 75,000 residents and your lot allows a single-family home, the city must allow accessory dwelling units on it.

The statute gave covered cities a deadline with real teeth. Under § 9-461.18(F), any covered municipality that failed to adopt conforming ADU regulations by **January 1, 2025** must allow ADUs on *all* of its residentially zoned lots **without limits**. That penalty is why every large Valley city moved quickly to adopt compliant ordinances in late 2024 — Phoenix, for example, adopted its text amendment (Z-TA-2-24) specifically to comply, per the [city's ADU page](https://www.phoenix.gov/administration/departments/pdd/residential-building/accessory-dwelling-units.html).

One year later, **HB 2928** ([Laws 2025, Chapter 217](https://www.azleg.gov/legtext/57leg/1r/laws/0217.htm), approved May 23, 2025) extended the same framework beyond city limits: it added **[A.R.S. § 11-810.01](https://www.azleg.gov/ars/11/00810-01.htm)**, which applies the ADU mandate to counties, with a compliance deadline of **January 1, 2026** and the same no-limits penalty for missing it. If you own property in unincorporated Maricopa or Pinal County, the law now reaches you too.

## What Counts as an ADU?

The statute's definition (§ 9-461.18(I)) is broader than most people expect. An **accessory dwelling unit** is a self-contained living unit on the same lot as a larger, primary single-family home, with its own sleeping area and its own sanitation facilities. A kitchen is allowed but not required by the definition. The unit can be:

- **Detached** — the classic backyard casita or guest house;
- **Attached** — built onto the main house with its own entrance, the in-law-suite pattern; or
- **Converted** — carved out of existing space, most often a garage.

Whatever you call it — casita, guest house, granny flat, in-law suite, garage apartment — if it's a second self-contained dwelling on a single-family lot, it's an ADU in the law's eyes, and the same rules apply. The one structural constant: the ADU is the *accessory*, meaning it sits on the same lot as a larger primary dwelling.

## Which Arizona Cities Must Allow ADUs?

Section 9-461.18(H) states the test in one line: the section "applies to a municipality with a population of more than seventy-five thousand persons." Here is how that shakes out across the East Valley and Phoenix metro communities we build in:

|---|---|---|
| Phoenix | **Yes** | Adopted conforming rules (Z-TA-2-24); allows *more* than the state floor — see [phoenix.gov](https://www.phoenix.gov/administration/departments/pdd/residential-building/accessory-dwelling-units.html) |
| Mesa | **Yes** | Conforming rules on the [city's ADU page](https://mesaaz.gov/Business-Development/Development-Services/Planning/Current-Planning/Accessory-Dwelling-Units): 5-ft side/rear setbacks, no added parking |
| Chandler | **Yes** | Well above the population threshold |
| Gilbert | **Yes** | Adopted conforming regulations; ADUs are a permitted use with no use permit, per the town's [staff report](https://gilbertaz.gov/home/showpublisheddocument/50285/638659944800900000) |
| Scottsdale | **Yes** | Adopted an ADU ordinance to meet the January 1, 2025 deadline |
| Tempe | **Yes** | Above the threshold |
| Queen Creek | **Unclear — ask the town** | 59,519 residents at the [2020 census](https://census.gov/quickfacts/fact/table/queencreektownarizona/PST045223); recent Census Bureau estimates exceed 75,000. Town rules govern unless and until the town is covered |
| San Tan Valley | **In transition — ask** | Incorporated as a town on September 17, 2025; previously governed by Pinal County's rules under § 11-810.01. Confirm current requirements with the town or county |
| Fountain Hills | **No** | Population well under 75,000 — the town sets its own ADU rules |
| Paradise Valley | **No** | Population well under 75,000 — town rules apply |

Two caveats worth knowing even inside covered cities. First, § 9-461.18(G) carves out certain land entirely: lots on tribal land, lots near a military airport or ancillary military facility (as defined in A.R.S. § 28-8461), and lots near certain public airports where the noise level exceeds 65 decibels. If you're under a flight path, check your parcel's noise-contour status before you plan. Second, a town below the threshold isn't ADU-hostile by default — it just writes its own rules, and several do allow casitas with their own standards.

And since 2025, the question "is my land covered?" no longer ends at city limits: on unincorporated county land, § 11-810.01 requires the county to allow ADUs on single-family lots, with the same unit counts and size floor described below.

## How Many ADUs Can I Build?

On any lot where a single-family home is allowed, a covered city must permit — as a matter of right — **at least one attached and one detached ADU** (§ 9-461.18(A)). On lots of **one acre or more**, the city must permit **one additional detached ADU**, provided at least one ADU on the lot is a *restricted-affordable dwelling unit* — a unit rented, under a deed restriction or development agreement, to households earning up to 80% of the area median income.

Three things to understand about those numbers:

1. **They are floors, not ceilings.** A city may allow more. Phoenix, for instance, permits two ADUs in addition to the primary dwelling on single-family lots, per its [official ADU page](https://www.phoenix.gov/administration/departments/pdd/residential-building/accessory-dwelling-units.html).
2. **"Permitted use" means no zoning gauntlet.** The statute defines it as approval without a public hearing, variance, conditional use permit, special permit, or special exception. Your neighbors don't get a vote on your casita.
3. **Permits still exist.** Plan review and building permits still apply, and the city may still check that a site plan conforms to its zoning ordinance. The law removed discretionary approval, not building safety review.

## How Big Can My ADU Be?

Covered cities must allow an ADU of at least **75% of the primary home's gross floor area, or 1,000 square feet, whichever is less** (§ 9-461.18(A)). "Gross floor area" means the interior habitable area of the dwelling. In practice:

| Main home (gross floor area) | 75% of main home | State-floor ADU allowance |
|---|---|---|
| 1,200 sq ft | 900 sq ft | **900 sq ft** (75% is the smaller number) |
| 1,600 sq ft | 1,200 sq ft | **1,000 sq ft** (the cap kicks in) |
| 2,400 sq ft | 1,800 sq ft | **1,000 sq ft** |

Again, that's the minimum cities must allow — and Phoenix goes well past it. Under the city's rules, an ADU may be up to 75% of the main house's gross floor area, **capped at 1,000 square feet on lots up to 10,000 square feet and at 3,000 square feet on larger lots** ([phoenix.gov](https://www.phoenix.gov/administration/departments/pdd/residential-building/accessory-dwelling-units.html)). On a big Phoenix lot with a large main house, that is genuinely house-sized square footage.

The practical limit on most lots isn't the statute — it's **lot coverage**. Your zoning district's maximum building coverage still applies, and on a compact lot the main house, patio, and pool may already consume most of it. This is the first thing we measure at a lot consultation, because it determines whether your 1,000-square-foot ambition is a drawing or a building.

## What Can't My City Require?

Section 9-461.18(B) reads like a list of every tactic cities historically used to make casitas impractical — and prohibits each one. A covered municipality may **not**:

- Require **additional parking** for the ADU, or accept payment in lieu of parking;
- Impose **rear or side setbacks greater than five feet** from the property line;
- Apply **height, setback, lot size, lot coverage, or frontage standards stricter** than those for single-family homes in the same zone;
- Require the ADU to **match the main house's exterior design, roof pitch, or finish materials**;
- Require any **familial, marital, employment, or other relationship** between the owner or occupant of the main house and the occupant of the ADU;
- **Prohibit long-term rental** of the main house or the ADU, or the advertising of either for long-term rental;
- Require **public street improvements** as a condition (except repairing street damage the ADU construction itself causes);
- Require a **restrictive covenant** on the ADU as a condition of a permit or license;
- Hold the ADU to a **commercial building code**; or
- Require **fire sprinklers** in the ADU (§ 9-461.18(D)).

If a covered city's counter staff tells you something on this list is required, the statute is on your side — politely, and in writing.

## Can I Rent Out My ADU?

This is the question behind most of the casitas we're asked about, and the answer splits cleanly in two.

**Long-term rental: protected.** A covered city cannot prohibit you from using the ADU — or the main house — as separately leased long-term rental housing, and it cannot prohibit you from advertising it (§ 9-461.18(B)). The statute defines *long-term rental* as a tenancy under a lease of **90 days or longer, or month-to-month**. A city also cannot require any relationship between you and your tenant, which is the provision that makes genuine rental income — not just housing relatives — a legal use.

**Short-term rental: a different statute, with one big catch.** Vacation and short-term rentals in Arizona are governed by **[A.R.S. § 9-500.39](https://www.azleg.gov/ars/9/00500-39.htm)**. Cities may not ban short-term rentals outright (§ 9-500.39(A)), but they may regulate them: local permits or licenses (fee capped at $250), emergency-contact registration, neighbor notification, liability insurance of at least $500,000 or equivalent marketplace coverage, and permit numbers in advertisements, among other measures listed in § 9-500.39(B).

The catch for ADU owners: § 9-500.39(B)(9) lets a city **require the owner to reside on the property** if an ADU on it is used as a vacation or short-term rental — but only where the unit's certificate of occupancy or similar final approval was issued **on or after September 14, 2024**. Units finaled on or before September 13, 2024 are exempt. In plain terms: if you're building a new casita in a city that adopts this requirement and plan to list it nightly on a rental platform, be prepared to live in the main house. If your plan is a 12-month lease, none of this applies to you.

## What Rules Still Apply?

The ADU law is a limit on city zoning power — not a free pass from everything else:

- **HOA covenants survive.** Section 9-461.18(C) expressly preserves private restrictive covenants. If your CC&Rs prohibit second dwellings, the state law does not override them. Read your HOA documents first; it's step one of our lot review.
- **Building, fire, and health codes apply** (§ 9-461.18(D)) — minus the two carve-outs above (no commercial code, no sprinkler mandate). The ADU is inspected like any habitable structure.
- **Permits and inspections are unchanged.** Plan review, building permits, trade inspections, and a final certificate of occupancy are all still part of the project.
- **Utility easements need written consent.** An ADU may not be built over a current or planned public utility easement without the written consent of every utility that uses or may use it (§ 9-461.18(E)). Your plat or survey shows these.
- **The exemption zones** — tribal land, military-airport vicinity, and 65+ decibel airport noise contours — sit outside the statute entirely (§ 9-461.18(G)).

## Is My Lot Eligible? A Five-Point Check

Before anyone draws a floor plan, run your property through this list — it's the same sequence we walk at a free consultation:

1. **Jurisdiction.** Is your lot inside a covered city (table above), in unincorporated county land under § 11-810.01, or in a smaller town with its own rules?
2. **Lot and home math.** Pull your lot size and your home's square footage from the county assessor's records. They set your state-floor size allowance and tell you whether the one-acre second-detached-unit provision is in play.
3. **HOA documents.** Read the CC&Rs for anything touching accessory structures, guest houses, or rentals. This is the most common project-killer, and the statute won't save you from it.
4. **Easements.** Check the recorded plat or a survey for utility easements crossing the buildable area — written utility consent is required to build over one, and routing around one is usually the better answer.
5. **Airport and overlay zones.** Confirm your parcel isn't in a military-airport vicinity or a 65+ dB public-airport noise contour, and check for any local overlay districts.

One more practical note from the field: on tight lots where coverage math is unforgiving, a **garage conversion** often succeeds where a detached casita can't — the footprint already exists, so lot coverage doesn't move. It's frequently the fastest path to a legal, rentable second unit.
