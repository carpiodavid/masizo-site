When a house gets too small, the usual advice is to move — and moving means transaction costs on both ends, a higher interest rate than the one you may be sitting on, new schools, and leaving a neighborhood you chose on purpose. A room addition is the other answer: keep the house, the street, and the school enrollment, and build the space you're missing onto the home you already own. ## The Test of a Good Addition: You Can't Find It

Everyone has seen a bad addition. The roofline that meets the original roof at an angle no architect would draw. The stucco patch in a slightly wrong texture. The step down at a doorway where two slabs never agreed on a height. Those tells do worse than look awkward — a visibly tacked-on room can work against a home's value instead of adding to it.

So we treat matching as a design requirement, not a finishing touch. Before we draw anything, we study how your house is built: the roof pitch and material, the exterior finish and its texture, the window style and trim, the interior floor heights. The addition is then engineered to continue those lines — roof planes that resolve naturally, exterior finish blended across the joint, and floors that meet flush. The goal is a room that a visitor, an appraiser, or a future buyer reads as original construction.

## What We Build

### Bedroom Additions

The most common call we get: the family outgrew the bedroom count, and nobody wants to change schools over it. We build bedroom additions as complete private spaces — many with an attached bathroom and a walk-in closet — placed where your lot and setbacks allow, and permitted and inspected like the habitable space they are. A well-planned bedroom suite can carry a house through a decade of family growth for far less upheaval than a move.

### Family Rooms, Dens, and Home Offices

Living-space additions are about how the house functions day to day: a family room big enough for everyone at once, a den that gives the household a quiet second living area, or a home office with a door that actually closes — a different thing entirely from a desk in the dining room. These rooms invite built-ins, media walls, and extra natural light, and we design that into the structure rather than bolting it on afterward.

### Additions Combined With a Remodel

An addition changes how the whole floor plan flows, and it's often the natural moment to fix what the original layout got wrong. Opening a wall between the new family room and a cramped kitchen, or pairing a bedroom addition with a [remodel](/services/home-remodeling) of the hall bath it will share, costs far less as one project than as two. We run the combined scope on a single contract — one crew, one schedule, one number to call — instead of leaving you to referee two contractors occupying the same house.

## The Parts You Don't See Are the Parts That Matter

An addition is structural work. It needs its own foundation, tied correctly to the existing slab; framing engineered to carry its roof and connect soundly to the original structure; and a roof tied in so the junction sheds water for decades, because in Arizona the monsoon will find any shortcut eventually. Our crew handles the foundation and framing ourselves — this is the skeleton of the project, and we don't subcontract our accountability for it.

Just as important, the new room has to work like the rest of the house. That means electrical extended with the panel's capacity confirmed, plumbing routed properly where a bathroom is involved, and HVAC that actually conditions the space — an addition that swelters every July because nobody sized the ductwork is a Valley cliché we refuse to build. We tie in all three systems as part of the base scope, not as change orders.

## Permits, HOAs, and Setbacks — Handled Before They're Problems

Every city we work in requires a permit for an addition, without exception. We prepare the plans and permit package, file them, answer the city's plan-review comments, and schedule every inspection — foundation, framing, systems, and final — so the project never sits waiting on paperwork. If you have an HOA, its architectural rules get checked at the consultation along with your lot's setback lines, because where the room *can* go is a fact to establish on day one, not week six.

## Living There While We Build

Most clients stay home during an addition, and we sequence the work to make that livable. The foundation, framing, roof, and exterior shell all go up outside your walls; your house stays closed, secured, and functioning. Only near the end do we open the shared wall to connect the new room — as late in the schedule as the work allows — and we contain dust at that opening while the connection is made. You'll know the plan and the timing of that step in advance, because surprises are for parties, not construction.

## What Shapes an Addition's Timeline and Budget

Two additions of identical square footage can be very different projects, and it helps to know why before you collect bids. On the schedule side, the big variables are permit review time in your city, the complexity of the roof tie-in, and whether the addition carries plumbing — a bedroom with a bathroom involves trades and inspections a den does not. On the budget side, the drivers are the foundation and structural work, how far electrical and HVAC have to travel from your existing systems, the exterior matching scope, and your finish selections inside the room. None of this is a reason for vague pricing: once we've walked your home, every one of those variables has an answer, and they all appear as plain line items in the written quote rather than as padding hidden in a lump sum. If the numbers point toward a garage conversion or a detached unit instead, we'll say so — that path is covered on our [ADU page](/services/adu-construction).

## Addition or ADU? The Honest Comparison

Some families come to us asking for an addition and leave planning a casita — or the reverse. The distinction is simple: **an addition makes your home bigger; an ADU is a second, self-contained dwelling on the same lot**, with its own entrance and bath, that can house a relative independently or earn long-term rent. Arizona law now requires many Valley cities to allow ADUs on single-family lots — our [Arizona ADU law guide](/guides/arizona-adu-law) explains the rules — and our [ADU construction](/services/adu-construction) page covers casitas, garage conversions, and in-law suites in depth. Because we build both, the consultation is a comparison, not a pitch: we'll lay out what each path costs and does on your lot, and you choose.
