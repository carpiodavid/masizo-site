## What to do in the first ten minutes

Before anything else, make the situation stop getting worse — but only if you can do it safely:

1. **Stop the water.** For a burst pipe or failed supply line, close the main shutoff valve, usually near the hose bib at the front of the house or in the garage. For a single fixture, the angle stop under the sink or behind the toilet may be enough.
2. **Cut power to the wet area.** If water is pooling near outlets, cords, or appliances, switch off the affected circuits at the breaker panel. If the panel itself is wet, leave it alone and stay clear.
3. **Call [[PHONE]].** Describe what you see. We will tell you what to do next and get a crew moving.

What you should not do is wait. Water keeps traveling after the leak stops — wicking up drywall, soaking baseboards, spreading under flooring into rooms that still look dry. The longer materials stay wet, the more has to be torn out, and the more likely mold becomes.

## Water damage restoration

In the East Valley, the water emergencies we see most often come from a handful of sources: supply lines and fittings that let go inside a wall, water heaters that fail, washing machine and dishwasher hoses, air-conditioner condensate lines that clog and overflow in the attic during the summer cooling season, and roof leaks that open up in monsoon storms.

Restoration professionals grade water losses by how contaminated the water is — from clean supply-line water, to gray water from appliances, to black water from sewage or outside flooding — and by how much of the structure it has saturated. Those gradings, set out in the IICRC's industry standards, determine what can be dried in place and what must be removed. Clean water caught quickly might mean drying equipment and patience; contaminated water or a long-unnoticed leak usually means controlled tear-out before drying begins.

Our sequence is the same either way: extract standing water, remove what cannot be saved, dry the structure with air movers and dehumidifiers, verify with moisture readings — then rebuild. If a roof leak caused it, we repair the roof too; see [our roofing services](/services/roofing).

## Mold removal

Mold needs moisture, a food source, and time — and a wall cavity that stayed wet after a slow leak provides all three. By the time mold shows on the painted surface, there is usually more of it behind the drywall, which is why wiping down a stain never solves the problem.

Proper remediation, following the IICRC's mold-specific standard, means three things done in order: contain the work area so spores do not spread through the house, remove the mold along with the materials it has colonized, and — most importantly — find and fix the moisture source that fed it. Remediation without a moisture fix is a countdown to the next outbreak. Because we are also a plumbing-capable general contractor and roofer, the source gets repaired on the same job, not referred out.

## Fire and smoke damage

Fire damage rarely stays where the fire was. Smoke and soot travel on air currents through the whole house, settling into textiles, cabinetry, and HVAC ductwork rooms away from the burn. A proper fire restoration therefore has two scopes: the structural one — tearing out and rebuilding what the fire took — and the cleaning one, removing soot and odor from everything the smoke touched.

We handle both, along with the pack-out of belongings that need off-site cleaning or safe storage while the rebuild proceeds. Rooms burned to the studs are reframed, re-insulated, rocked, and finished. Because the rebuild runs under our general contracting license, this is also the natural moment to improve a layout rather than merely put back what was there — many families use the reconstruction to fix what they never liked about the room. ## Working with your insurance company

Most restoration jobs involve an insurance claim, and claims run on documentation. From the first site visit we photograph the damage, record moisture readings, and keep a written scope of what was removed and why. We share that record with your insurer and meet the adjuster on site when your claim is assigned.

Two honest notes, because you deserve straight answers on a bad day. First, your insurance company — not your contractor — decides what your policy covers; anyone who promises you a coverage outcome is guessing with your money. Second, speed matters to claims as much as to the structure: reporting promptly and mitigating the damage quickly is generally expected under most policies, which is one more reason not to let water sit over a weekend.
