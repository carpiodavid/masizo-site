## Commercial General Contracting

Commercial construction is a different discipline from residential, even when the trades overlap. The codes are stricter in different places — accessibility, fire and life safety, exiting, mechanical ventilation — the plan-review process is more involved, and the stakes are different too, because every week of construction is a week the space isn't producing revenue. The contractor's job is therefore as much about sequencing and communication as it is about building: a commercial client needs to know, at any moment, what's done, what's next, and what date the doors open.

That's the standard we run our commercial projects to. One licensed general contractor ([[LICENSE_GC]]) accountable for the scope, a written price before work begins, permits and inspections managed inside the project schedule rather than around it, and changes priced in writing before they're built. It's the same discipline that has kept us in business for [[YEARS]] — applied to buildings where downtime is money.

## Tenant Improvements and Build-Outs

A tenant improvement is the construction that turns a leased shell or a previous tenant's space into *your* space — new walls and layout, flooring, ceilings, lighting, electrical and data distribution, restroom and break-area work, finishes, and whatever the use demands. TI projects live at the intersection of three documents: the lease, the landlord's requirements, and the building code. Getting them to agree is half the job.

If you're a tenant, we can start from whatever you're holding — a landlord work letter, a space plan from a designer, or nothing but the lease and a layout in your head — and turn it into a scoped, priced, permitted project. If you're a landlord or property manager preparing a space between tenants, the same crew and process apply: scope the turn, price it in writing, build it, and hand back a space that shows well and passes inspection.

Two pieces of advice we give every TI client before a dollar is spent:

- **Read the lease's construction provisions first.** Who pays for what, what requires landlord approval, and what must be restored at the end of the term all shape the smart scope. Build what the lease rewards.
- **Respect the plan-review clock.** Commercial permits pass through more review stations than residential ones. The schedule has to start from permit submittal, not from demolition day — a contractor who promises otherwise is borrowing time from the end of your project.

## Office Remodels

Not every commercial project is a new space. Plenty of our commercial conversations start with an office that's simply outdated — a layout from a different era of how the business works, worn finishes that undercut the company in front of clients, lighting and break areas that make recruiting harder than it needs to be. An office remodel updates the space you already occupy: reconfigured walls and workareas, refreshed finishes, updated lighting and electrical for how people actually plug in today, and restrooms and common areas brought up to the standard the rest of the remodel sets.

The defining constraint of an office remodel is that the business usually can't stop for it. How the work gets phased — what happens in which order, and when the disruptive stages land — is a scheduling conversation we have with you up front, built around how your operation runs, and it's settled in the plan before crews arrive rather than improvised week to week.

Fire losses compound the same way: beyond the burn damage, smoke and the water used to stop the fire keep doing harm until mitigation starts.
